# LAN, WAN, and MAN

Computer networks connect devices so they can share data and resources. LAN, MAN, and WAN classify networks mainly by their geographical coverage.

## LAN: Local Area Network

A **LAN** connects devices in a small area such as a home, office, school, laboratory, or building. It usually offers high speed and is managed by one organization.

**Examples:** Office computers connected to shared storage, printers, and a local database.

## MAN: Metropolitan Area Network

A **MAN** connects multiple LANs across a city or large campus. It is larger than a LAN but smaller than a WAN.

**Examples:** A university connecting buildings across a city or a city authority connecting offices.

## WAN: Wide Area Network

A **WAN** connects networks across countries or continents using leased lines, fiber, satellites, or the internet. The internet is the largest example of a WAN.

## Comparison

| Feature | LAN | MAN | WAN |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coverage | Building or small site | City or large campus | Country or global |
| Speed | Usually high | High to moderate | Varies by connection |
| Ownership | Usually one organization | Organization or provider | Multiple providers |
| Example | Office network | City university network | Company branches worldwide |

## Role in Data Analytics

LANs support local databases, file sharing, and dashboards. MANs connect analytics resources across sites in a city. WANs allow analysts to access cloud data warehouses, remote databases, APIs, and dashboards from different locations. Network speed, reliability, and security affect data-pipeline performance.

## Security

Organizations should use access controls, encryption, firewalls, secure Wi-Fi, monitoring, and backups. Sensitive analytics data should not be exposed to unauthorized networks.

## Conclusion

LAN, MAN, and WAN describe networks by size. Together, they provide the connectivity required to collect, process, store, and share data.
